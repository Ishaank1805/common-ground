# Existing solutions: what is out there, and where Common Ground fits

Feedback item 7 asked us to research existing solutions. We looked at four groups: AI apps for rehearsing hard conversations, tools that coach people during live conversations, programmes that build girls' negotiation and life skills, and digital tools for young people in India. Research done in November 2026.

## 1. AI apps for rehearsing hard conversations

Many apps now let people practise a difficult conversation with an AI that plays the other person, then give feedback. Examples include TalkSimulator, Simuverse, Hard Talk Roleplay Coach, VirtualSpeech, Yoodli, MindTools AI Conversations and TrueTalkAI. A September 2026 roundup lists Yoodli, VirtualSpeech and similar tools as the leading options, and notes that Yoodli is aimed at workplace conversations and runs in the browser rather than on a phone.

What they do well: they lower anxiety by letting people try out words before the real moment.

What they miss for our participants: almost all are built for the workplace (raises, performance reviews, layoffs), work in English, often need a paid subscription, and stop at rehearsal. None support the real conversation as it happens, none has a safety layer for a conversation that turns threatening, and none was designed with an NGO for young women negotiating their education with family.

What we took from them: preparation builds confidence, which is why Common Ground starts with a plan in her own words. A short "practise your opening line" step is a natural next feature.

## 2. Tools that coach people during a live conversation

Real-time coaching is common in call centres and sales. Dialpad's Live Coach Cards, Balto, Cogito and Revenue.io listen to a live call and show short guidance on the agent's screen, such as how to answer an objection, without interrupting the conversation. Some, like Dialpad's, are triggered by keywords. Revenue.io notes that new staff rely on the prompts most in their first two to three months. Picovoice offers coaching that runs on the device to keep voice data local.

What they miss for our participants: they are built for employers to steer employees, often with supervisors watching, and they prompt constantly. That is the opposite of what the NGO asked for: support that is optional, passive, minimal and fading, so that girls build their own ability.

What we took from them:
- Show help on screen without interrupting: our quiet prompts never beep, vibrate or pop up.
- Help should fade as experience grows: Common Ground suggests "As we go" only for her first three recorded conversations, then "When I ask".
- Keep voice processing close: our experimental voice matching runs on our own server, not a third-party service.
- Unlike these tools, nobody watches her: she alone decides whether prompts are on.

## 3. Programmes that build girls' negotiation and life skills

This is the NGO's own field, and the evidence is encouraging.

- **Negotiation training (Zambia, Ashraf and colleagues).** A randomised trial found that teaching adolescent girls negotiation skills improved their educational outcomes over the following three years. It worked better than giving girls a safe space with female mentors, or information about the value of education, which suggests the negotiation skills themselves made the difference.
- **Girls' Education Program, Room to Read (Rajasthan).** A randomised trial with about 2,500 girls found stronger life skills, more future planning, more say over important life decisions, and less dropout through the move to high school.
- **PAnKH (Rajasthan).** The programme alone had no significant effect on girls' life skills, but girls' mental health improved when the wider community was engaged too. Families and communities matter, not just the girl.
- **Project Lehar, Aga Khan Foundation (Uttar Pradesh and Bihar).** Combines schooling support, vocational training and life skills, including negotiation; 42% of girls reported more say in household decisions.

What this means for Common Ground: negotiation skills can change real outcomes, but they are taught in group sessions and then used alone, at home. Common Ground supports exactly that gap: preparing for a specific family conversation, holding it with optional support, and reflecting afterwards. It complements the NGO's sessions rather than replacing them, and the follow-up and anonymous insights features connect what happens at home back to the NGO.

## 4. Digital tools for young people in India

**SnehAI** (Population Foundation of India) is the closest digital example. It was described as the first Hinglish AI chatbot, co-created with young people as a private, non-judgemental space for sensitive topics. A peer-reviewed case study found that about half of incoming messages were deeply personal questions, reported 8.2 million messages over five months, and counted over 71,000 visits to its helplines feature.

What we took from it:
- Hinglish matters: Common Ground offers English, Hindi and Hinglish prompts.
- Help must be easy to find: our helplines sit one tap away, behind "More help".
- Co-design with young people: our next step is a pilot with real participants.
- Reach: WhatsApp is how SnehAI reaches many young people, which is worth considering later.

## Where Common Ground fits

We found no tool that does all of the following together:
- plans a real conversation with family, in her own words;
- offers optional, quiet, fading support during that conversation;
- keeps a discreet safety response for conversations that turn threatening;
- supports reflection afterwards, and follow-up when the conversation has not happened yet;
- is built with an NGO for young women in India, with privacy as a default.

Rehearsal apps stop before the conversation, live coaching tools are built for employers, life-skills programmes stop at the classroom door, and youth chatbots answer questions rather than support a conversation. Common Ground sits in the gap between them.
