Common Ground

Helping young women prepare for, hold and reflect on the family conversations that shape their future.

[Presenter name] · [Date]

Introduce yourself and the project in one sentence: Common Ground is a phone app that helps a young woman prepare for an important conversation with her family, such as asking to continue her studies, hold that conversation with optional quiet support, and reflect on it afterwards. It was built with Umeed for Women by a student team at IIIT Hyderabad (Ishaan, Durga, Ananth and Atharv). Say that everything in it is designed around one principle the NGO set: support must be optional, quiet, minimal and fading, so that the girls build their own confidence rather than depending on an app.

Why it matters

The decisions that shape her future are often made at home

Whether she continues her studies can depend on one conversation with her family. These conversations are hard to start, easy to lose track of, and rarely prepared for.

The evidence

In a randomised trial, teaching adolescent girls negotiation skills improved their education outcomes over the next three years, more than mentoring or information alone.

The gap

Skills are taught in group sessions, but she uses them alone, at home, with the people who matter most to her.

Source: Ashraf and colleagues, negotiation training trial, CEPR Discussion Paper 12939.

Start with the participant, not the technology. For many young women the decision about further study is made in a single conversation with parents. Point to the evidence: a randomised trial found that negotiation training improved girls' educational outcomes over three years, and it worked better than mentoring or information about the value of education. So the skill itself matters. Then name the gap Common Ground fills: Umeed for Women's sessions build these skills in a group, but the real conversation happens later, at home, without support. Common Ground is for that moment.

How it works

Three steps, all in her own words

1 · Before

Prepare

She writes who she is talking to, what she wants to achieve and what she expects. The app turns this into a plan she can edit.

2 · During

Record

She records the conversation on her phone. If she chooses, quiet prompts help her stay on track.

3 · After

Reflect

She judges how each goal went, in her own words. If the conversation hasn’t happened yet, the app helps her get ready to try again.

She can stop at any step. Nothing is shared unless she chooses.

Walk through the three steps quickly; the next slides go deeper. Emphasise that her own words and her own judgement are at the centre: she writes the goals, she edits the plan, she assesses how it went. The AI helps but never decides for her. Mention that it works on an ordinary phone in a browser, in English, Hindi or Hinglish for the prompts.

Step 1 · Prepare

A plan she owns, built from her own goals

What she writes

Who she will speak to, the topic, what she wants to achieve, what might go well and what might get in the way.

What the app asks

Up to three short questions to understand her situation better, never more.

What she gets

A step-by-step plan with talking points and example wording, which she can change freely.

Example: “I want to do a master’s degree in another city”, with goals about explaining why it matters, reassuring her parents about safety, and agreeing a next step.

Use the example on the slide to make it concrete. Point out that the plan is a suggestion she edits, not a script; her words stay hers. The app asks at most three clarifying questions, so preparation takes minutes, not an hour. If you can, show the Prepare screen live on a phone here.

Step 2 · During the conversation

Quiet prompts, only if she wants them

Default

No prompts

Just the recording. Every conversation starts here.

On request

When I ask

She taps once; her next planned point appears straight away, then a short cue based on the conversation so far.

Light touch

As we go

A cue appears only when it would really help, such as an unanswered concern or drifting off topic.

Never a beep, vibration or pop-up. Help fades: after her first three conversations the app suggests “When I ask” instead of “As we go”.

This answers the NGO's first piece of feedback: support should be optional, passive, minimal and fading. Prompts are off for every new conversation; she switches them on herself, after a short explanation of what is sent to the AI. The prompts are short, appear silently on screen, and are based on what was actually said, not just the plan. Example: if her father raises the cost, a cue might suggest she offer to look at the fees together. Prompts can be in English, Hindi or Hinglish. The fading is deliberate: the goal is that one day she does not need the app.

Safety first

If a conversation turns threatening, her screen shows only “Take a break”.

The helplines, 112 and the Women Helpline on 181, are one tap away behind “More help”, so family members looking at the screen see nothing alarming.

In testing, threats triggered this every time (12 out of 12), and ordinary disagreement or interruption never did (0 out of 12).

This is the most important design decision. If someone threatens or intimidates her, the app shows a calm, neutral card: take a break and continue another time with someone you trust. It is deliberately discreet, because a family member may be looking at the phone. The helplines sit behind More help and are listed in full after the conversation. Explain how it is made reliable: the AI must first answer a separate yes or no question about threats, and the server itself enforces the safety message when the answer is yes. In testing this caught every threat, including calmly spoken ones, and never fired on normal disagreement. We need the NGO's help to translate this message into Hindi with the right wording.

Step 3 · After

Reflect, or get ready to try again

If it happened

She rates each goal as achieved, partly achieved or not achieved, and writes what went differently from what she expected. An AI summary is optional.

If it hasn’t happened yet

She says what got in the way, such as nerves or no right moment, and gets advice for that reason. If it didn’t feel safe, she sees the helplines.

When she comes back

A gentle check-in asks whether her planned conversation happened, on the day she chose to try again.

Reflection is in her own words; the app never grades her. The follow-up answers the NGO's feedback that participants need different support depending on whether the conversation happened. The advice for each reason is fixed text written in advance, so Umeed for Women can review and change it; no AI is involved there. If she says it did not feel safe, the app tells her she does not have to have the conversation and shows the helplines.

Privacy by design

Her conversations stay hers

Her own private account

No one else, including the NGO, can open her plans, recordings or reflections.

Built for shared phones

No “keep me signed in”; she is signed out after 30 minutes without activity.

She can delete anything

A conversation, a recording, or her whole account and everything in it.

Counts, only with consent

If she opts in, the NGO sees anonymous totals from at least five people, never her words.

Reassure the audience that privacy is the default, not an add-on. Each participant has her own account; the NGO cannot read anyone's conversations. Because phones are often shared, there is no keep-me-signed-in option. She can delete anything at any time. The anonymous insights answer the NGO's feedback about understanding participants' needs: only if she opts in, only counts such as how many conversations happened and what got in the way, nothing until at least five people share, and small numbers hidden so no one can be identified. AI is used only when she asks for a plan or summary, or switches prompts on.

Honest limits

What we have not tested yet

Real participants

All tests so far used scripted conversations. A small pilot with real participants is the most important next step.

Every phone

Tested in Chrome; iPhones and slower phones and networks still need checking.

Hinglish prompts

Hindi works well; Hinglish wording needs review by someone who speaks as participants do.

Telling voices apart

Offered at sign-up and optional, but still to be tested on real families’ voices.

Being open about limits builds trust. Nothing has been tested with real participants yet, which is why we are asking for a pilot. Phones: tested in Chrome on a laptop at phone size; iPhones and low-end Android phones need checking. Hinglish: prompts in Hindi were correct, but Hinglish wording sometimes repeated English sentences. Telling voices apart: she can register her voice at sign-up so the live transcript marks her lines as "You" and others as Person 1, 2; it worked in our tests with synthetic voices, but relatives can sound alike, so it must be tested on consented recordings of real families before anyone relies on it. One goal type, agreeing a next step, is also not yet tracked consistently.

Next steps

What we need from Umeed for Women

  1. A small pilot with participants who want to try it, and a consent approach for anyone under 18, since India’s data protection rules require a parent’s or guardian’s consent.
  2. Hindi wording for the safety message and the follow-up advice, approved by the NGO.
  3. How long recordings are kept, and what participants should tell family before recording.
  4. Who at the NGO sees anonymous insights, and where the app is hosted.
End with concrete asks. The pilot is the priority: a handful of participants who volunteer, with the NGO's facilitators nearby. Be direct about the legal point: India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025 require verifiable consent from a parent or guardian before processing data of anyone under 18, and most of those obligations apply from mid-2027. That is sensitive here, because the conversation is often with those same parents, so options include starting with participants aged 18 and over, or handling consent through the NGO's enrolment, with legal advice. The other decisions are in the privacy and consent summary we have shared.
